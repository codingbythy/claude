# Autumn Rain – Mr. Fox: Parts 4–6 (scripts)

Each part opens cold for a first-time viewer, puts its hook in the first sentence, and ends on a turn. Part 6 closes the story.

## Part 4 – The Photographs

Foxes do not live for fifty years. You know that. You looked it up twice. But the fox in your grandmother’s photograph has the same white tip on his tail. The same way of sleeping, with his nose tucked under. So you spend the evening on the floor, going through her desk. Receipts. Seed catalogues. The birthday cards you sent, and the gaps where you forgot. And more photographs. Nineteen seventy-four. Nineteen eighty-nine. Two thousand and six. Her hair goes grey. The armchair changes colour. The fox never changes at all. On the back of every one, in her handwriting, the same four words. He came back again. Mr. Fox hasn’t moved. But you are fairly sure he is listening. And at the very bottom of the drawer, there is an envelope with your name on it.

## Part 5 – The Letter

Your grandmother’s last letter begins with an apology. “I’m sorry about the roof,” she wrote. “And about your cousins.” You laugh, and then you don’t. She knew exactly what they would say. She left it to you anyway. “They would have sold it by spring. You were the only one who ever stayed for a second cup of tea.” You think of the visits you cancelled. The calls you kept short. She doesn’t mention those. “I was your age when I came here. No money, no plan, and a winter coming. He was on the doorstep the first night. He is not a pet. He comes with the house, to whoever needs it as much as it needs them. Leave his door unlatched. And when the first frost comes, follow him.” You fold the letter. Outside, the rain has stopped. The glass is turning white.

## Part 6 – The First Frost

On the night of the first frost, the armchair was empty. His little door was still swinging. You pulled a coat over your pyjamas and followed the paw prints, down the garden, past the birch, to a shed you had never managed to open. Tonight, the door stood ajar. Inside, shelves of paper envelopes, in her handwriting. Seeds. Bulbs. Fifty autumns, saved and labelled. And one envelope set apart. “For whoever comes next. Plant these when you are ready to stay.” Your cousins called in the spring. The market had changed, they said. They had an offer for you. A generous one. You looked out at the garden, coming up green in rows you had planted yourself. “It’s not for sale.” That evening, you came in from the rain, and set your umbrella on the counter. Someone was already sitting in your armchair. You wouldn’t have it any other way.
